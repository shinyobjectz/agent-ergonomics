# Tells: patterns that mark text as generated

A reader who spots one of these starts reading the rest as filler. Fix a tell by rewriting the sentence around what
it should say. Swapping one word for its synonym leaves the pattern in place, and the reader still sees it.

Not every instance is wrong. A real list of three is fine, and so is a dash where it helps. The problem is the
reflex: the pattern used because it's the default shape, not because the content has that shape.

## Words and phrases

- **Inflated vocabulary:** seamless, robust, powerful, effortless, cutting-edge, game-changing, revolutionary,
  innovative, comprehensive, holistic, dynamic, vibrant, rich, elevate, empower, unlock, unleash, supercharge,
  streamline, leverage, harness, delve, navigate (for anything but navigation), landscape, realm, tapestry, journey,
  ecosystem (for a product), testament, pivotal, crucial, paramount.
- **Throat-clearing openers:** "Let's dive in", "Here's the thing", "In today's fast-paced world", "When it comes
  to", "It's no secret that", "At its core", "Simply put".
- **Emphasis crutches:** "It's important to note", "It's worth mentioning", "Notably", "Importantly", "Crucially",
  "Indeed", "Truly", "Absolutely".
- **Servile or chatty framing:** "Great question", "Certainly!", "I hope this helps", "Feel free to", "Happy to help",
  "Let me know if you need anything else".
- **Vague attribution:** "Experts agree", "Studies show", "Many users find", with no source.
- **Hedges stacked on hedges:** "may potentially", "could possibly help to", "in some cases might".

## Structures

- **"Not X, but Y" and "It's not just X, it's Y".** Contrasting against a claim nobody made. State Y.
- **The reflex triplet.** Three adjectives, three benefits, three short sentences, every time. Use the real number of
  things. Two is often stronger.
- **Fragment drama.** "Fast. Simple. Yours." and the one-line paragraph that lands a punchline. Fine once in a
  headline, tiring as a habit.
- **The rhetorical question setup.** "So what does this mean for you?" followed by the answer. Give the answer.
- **The colon reveal.** "The result: a faster workflow." "Here's why: …" Say the sentence plainly.
- **The -ing tail.** "…, making it easier than ever to collaborate." "…, highlighting the importance of design." A
  clause that adds significance without adding a fact. Cut it, or turn it into a concrete claim.
- **Significance inflation.** Calling ordinary things pivotal, a testament, a turning point, or part of a broader
  trend.
- **Narrator from a distance.** "Users often struggle with…", "Nobody designed this…". Put the reader in it: "You
  open the file and…"
- **Things doing people's verbs.** "The data tells a story", "the feature empowers teams". Name who does what.
- **Recap endings.** "In summary", "Ultimately", "At the end of the day", or a closing paragraph that restates the
  piece. End on the last thing worth saying.
- **Uniform rhythm.** Every sentence the same length, every paragraph three sentences, every section the same shape.

## Formatting

- **Bold confetti:** bolding phrases in every sentence until nothing stands out.
- **Headings for everything:** a heading over every two sentences, or headings in Title Case Throughout.
- **Lists that should be sentences:** an argument broken into bullets, each starting with a bolded label and a colon,
  where the connections between points were the point.
- **Emoji as section markers** (🚀 ✨ 💡) in text that isn't casual.
- **Em dashes everywhere.** One in a while is punctuation. Several per paragraph is a signature. Use a comma, a
  colon, parentheses or a new sentence.
- **Markdown where it won't render:** asterisks and pound signs in an email, a commit subject or a UI string.

## Content

- **Generic examples:** "Acme Corp", "John Doe", "a user wants to…". Use a real, specific case.
- **Fake specificity:** numbers or names that sound precise but weren't checked.
- **Balanced-to-a-fault:** "There are pros and cons", "It depends on your needs", when the reader asked for a
  recommendation. Give one.
- **Explaining the obvious:** defining terms the reader certainly knows, or describing what a screenshot already
  shows.
- **Covering every angle:** an accessibility note, a security note and a performance note attached by reflex rather
  than because they apply.
